# Enduring Technology Spillovers and Direct Public Returns

**Generational Capital Investment (GCI) — Apollo research note**  
**Status:** Working framework; historical findings and counterfactual claims remain separate  
**Date:** 2026-09-25

## The new research question

Apollo is a test of whether a modest, declining participation right could have returned some commercial proceeds to a public fund for the next generational project. The IBM investigation reveals a related result worth documenting independently:

> A national mission can create or accelerate useful technologies and capabilities that remain in the private economy for decades, even when the evidence does not support a financial claim on every downstream product that uses them.

This note extends [Apollo as the Generational Investment Litmus Test](APOLLO_AS_GENERATIONAL_INVESTMENT_LITMUS_TEST.md). It does not replace the original direct-return question. It establishes a second research question: **What enduring private-sector economic capacity did the public mission create or accelerate?** “Enduring” means observed over a documented period; it does not predict that a technology will literally last forever.

## Two separate ledgers

| Ledger | What it measures | Examples | Treatment in the GCI calculation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Enduring economic capacity and indirect return** | Mission-enabled technology, skills, methods, suppliers, standards, and useful products that persist outside the mission | A commercial software product with Apollo origins; a specialized workforce or manufacturing process later used elsewhere | Document and, where possible, estimate economic significance. Do **not** count industry sales, GDP, or tax receipts as cash paid to a GCI fund. |
| **Direct public financial return** | Money or financial interests that actually flowed, or under an explicitly modeled GCI contract *could have flowed*, to the public | License fees, royalties, revenue participation, equity proceeds | Keep actual receipts and hypothetical GCI receipts separate. Count only a defined qualifying commercial base and stated terms. |

A case can be important in the first ledger and yield no defensible amount in the second. Neither result cancels the other. The Apollo portfolio must also retain its full historical investment denominator under the rules in the initiation document.

## The IBM pivot: three different histories

### 1. IBM's mainframe architecture: private development, Apollo use

IBM began planning System/360 in 1961 and introduced it in 1964. IBM describes the project as a roughly $5 billion, four-year company gamble. Apollo Mission Control later used five System/360 Model 75 machines. Modern IBM Z systems are architectural descendants of System/360, but that platform lineage **does not establish that Apollo originated the architecture**. [IBM System/360 history](https://www.ibm.com/history/system-360); [IBM Apollo history](https://www.ibm.com/history/apollo); [IBM architecture overview](https://www.ibm.com/docs/en/linux-on-systems?topic=findings-overview).

**Working classification:** Important procurement, deployment, and mission-use evidence; no claim on IBM's entire mainframe lineage merely because Apollo used it. Apollo-specific improvements require their own traced records.

### 2. IMS: a mission-driven product with documented persistence

IBM traces the Information Control System/Data Language Interface (ICS/DL/I) to an Apollo contractor's need to track millions of spacecraft parts and engineering changes. IBM, Rockwell, and Caterpillar contributed to the original system. IBM announced the commercial IMS product for System/360 in 1968. IBM still describes IMS as used for high-volume business transactions, and its IMS 15.6 documentation lists IBM z17 as a supported processor. These sources establish an Apollo-origin requirement, a commercialization step, and continuing product support. They do **not**, by themselves, establish the precise Apollo funding share, ownership terms, IMS revenue, or an actual public royalty. [IBM IMS history](https://www.ibm.com/history/information-management-system); [IBM IMS 15.6 processor requirements](https://www.ibm.com/docs/en/ims/15.6.0?topic=requirements-processor).

**Working classification:** Strong candidate for both ledgers. Quantifying a hypothetical direct return still requires contract and rights evidence, a defensible commercial revenue base, private-contribution allocation, and participation terms. IMS software revenue must not be inferred from IBM Z hardware revenue.

### 3. RTOS/360: Apollo-era innovation, downstream transfer still open

NASA published a 1969 technical brief on Real-Time Operating System/360 describing capabilities including flexible data routing, a device-independent display interface, storage protection, and data queuing. This establishes a documented Apollo-era technical subject for follow-up. It does **not** establish that a particular feature entered later IBM commercial operating systems or produced identifiable sales. [NASA technical brief MSC-12148](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19690000381).

**Working classification:** Research candidate. Trace specific features, code, personnel, documentation, or contracts forward before entering any commercial value in either ledger.

## A current-dollar boundary check

IBM reported **$15.718 billion of total Infrastructure revenue in 2025**, including **$10.618 billion of Hybrid Infrastructure**. IBM reported **51.7% year-over-year IBM Z revenue growth**, but did not report a separate IBM Z dollar total in that passage. This shows that the mainframe family remains economically active; it is **not** an Apollo-attributable revenue figure or a GCI royalty base. Any derived IBM Z estimate must be labeled as an estimate and shown with its inputs. [IBM 2025 Annual Report, Management Discussion](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/51143/000005114326000010/ibm-20251231_d2.htm).

The stronger finding is narrower: **an Apollo-origin commercial software product remains supported on a modern descendant of the platform used in Apollo, while the platform itself has a separate private origin.** The distinction makes the evidence more useful.

## Outcome categories for each case

These are findings to test, not labels that assume a return:

1. **Purchase of an independently developed product:** Mission benefit and possible wider spillover; a downstream financial participation claim needs additional evidence.
2. **Mission-created or materially improved commercial product:** Trace funding, contributors, rights, commercialization, and a properly bounded revenue stream; potentially strong GCI counterfactual.
3. **Joint public-private advancement:** Identify each party's contribution and consider a smaller or narrower participation base.
4. **Diffuse capacity:** Skills, reliability practices, suppliers, production methods, or standards may generate long-lived value without an identifiable royalty base.
5. **Unresolved transfer:** The mission work is documented, but the claimed path to a later commercial product remains unproven.
6. **Contrary finding:** A proposed Apollo lineage is refuted or too weak to retain. Record the correction rather than forcing a connection.

The same case may have more than one outcome: IBM's System/360 use, IMS productization, and RTOS/360 research lead to different conclusions.

## Minimum record for a defensible claim

For every claimed enduring capability or financial opportunity, record:

| Field | Question to answer |
| --- | --- |
| Mission contribution | What specific Apollo requirement, money, procurement, or test environment mattered? |
| Preexisting private work | What existed before the public mission, and who financed it? |
| Transfer mechanism | What identifiable product, process, know-how, personnel, or rights crossed into commercial use? |
| Persistence | What dated evidence establishes actual use or support in later generations? |
| Competing explanations | Could the same product or capability have emerged independently or from another government program? |
| Economic scope | What product-specific revenue is documented? Distinguish it from segment and company revenue. |
| Rights and receipts | What did the public actually own or receive? What did contracts permit? |
| Counterfactual | Under what proposed GCI terms would a payment arise, from which base, for how long, and with what decay? |
| Confidence | Which links are documented, inferred, estimated, or unverified? |

**Decision rule:** Architectural ancestry, shared corporate ownership, general influence, or contemporaneous participation is insufficient by itself to assign revenue to Apollo. A documented technology path does not by itself create a historical legal entitlement. A GCI payment is always a stated counterfactual unless an actual payment record exists.

## Next IBM research tasks

1. Trace RTOS/360 features individually into later commercial IBM software using contemporary IBM/NASA documentation; preserve negative results.
2. Reconstruct IMS's contracting and rights history among NASA, North American Aviation/Rockwell, IBM, and Caterpillar. Identify who paid for which development work.
3. Seek product-specific IMS financial evidence. If unavailable, leave the revenue base unknown and use transparent scenarios only after the rights and lineage work.
4. Add the IBM platform, IMS, and RTOS/360 as separate records in the Apollo derivative portfolio so their revenue and attribution cannot be combined accidentally.

## Working conclusion

The IBM evidence already supports a meaningful proposition: a demanding public mission can help bring a commercially durable capability into being. It supports a **specific, researchable** GCI question for IMS, and a **separate, unproven** question for RTOS/360. It does not support treating the modern IBM Z business as Apollo's invention. This is how the Apollo litmus test should handle pivots: preserve the observed economic legacy, narrow the financial claim to what the evidence can bear, and allow a finding of substantial public spillover with no quantified direct return.
