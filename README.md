# Generational Capital Investment

## An Open Economic Hypothesis

**Generational Capital Investment** is an open, nonpartisan economic hypothesis exploring whether the United States can preserve competitive capitalism while adding a professionally managed, long-horizon public investment system designed to build productive national assets for future generations.

This is **not a finished policy proposal**.

It is an idea being released publicly so economists, investors, entrepreneurs, legal scholars, policymakers, social scientists, fiscal experts, and citizens across the political spectrum can challenge it, improve it, or prove parts of it wrong.

## The Starting Premise

Capitalism remains the strongest known engine for innovation, competition, entrepreneurship, and wealth creation.

But private capital often operates on time horizons that do not align with the long-term needs of society.

Markets are very good at asking:

**What can produce an attractive return soon enough?**

A nation must also ask:

**What should America own, know, and be capable of 20, 30, or 50 years from now?**

Generational Capital Investment explores whether America needs a second investment clock — one measured in generations rather than quarters.

## The Core Principle

# If public capital takes risk, the public should share in the return.

When private investors take risk, private investors should receive the reward.

When taxpayers provide capital, guarantees, research funding, infrastructure, or other meaningful economic support, the hypothesis asks whether the public should retain an appropriate economic interest in the value that support helps create.

Possible forms of public return could include:

* Equity
* Royalties
* Licensing income
* Warrants
* Dividends
* Revenue participation
* Infrastructure income
* Other forms of long-term public ownership

The objective is not to replace capitalism.

The objective is to determine whether public investment can complement private markets by providing patient capital for national priorities that may require decades to mature.

## Why Explore This?

The hypothesis asks whether long-horizon public investment could eventually help the United States:

* Build strategic national capabilities
* Create productive public assets
* Generate long-term public investment income
* Strengthen the national balance sheet
* Encourage investment in infrastructure, science, energy, health, technology, and human capital
* Leave future generations more assets and fewer liabilities
* Create greater shared economic interest among American citizens

## The Social Hypothesis

Generational Capital Investment also asks a broader question:

**Can shared investment create shared purpose?**

A country whose citizens collectively own productive assets may develop a stronger sense that individual prosperity and national prosperity are connected.

This does not assume that economic security eliminates political division, prejudice, or social conflict.

It asks whether greater shared ownership, long-term security, and visible common purpose could help create healthier conditions for democratic society.

## What This Is Not

Generational Capital Investment is not:

* A proposal to replace capitalism
* A proposal for government to manage private companies
* A proposal for politicians to select individual corporate winners
* A plan to privatize Social Security
* A claim that investment returns alone can solve the national debt
* A finished economic doctrine

It is a hypothesis.

## The Critical Governance Principle

Elected government may establish broad national missions.

**Politicians should not select individual investments.**

Any viable system would require strong institutional safeguards separating democratic goal-setting from professional capital allocation.

The model succeeds only if public investment can be protected from:

* Political favoritism
* Crony capitalism
* Lobbying capture
* Pay-to-play influence
* Hidden financial interests
* Permanent support for failed investments

## Pressure Test the Hypothesis

We are specifically asking experts and critics to challenge the idea.

Among the unresolved questions:

### Political Firewall

How can long-term public capital be insulated from short-term political influence while remaining democratically accountable?

### Seed Capital

How could such a fund be created without worsening the fiscal problem it is intended to help address?

### Competitive Neutrality

How can public capital participate in economic upside without crowding out private investment or distorting competitive markets?

### Mission Durability

How can 20–50 year national missions survive changes in political leadership while still allowing future generations to change direction?

### Public Return

How should financial, strategic, and social returns be measured and allocated?

### Failure Discipline

How should unsuccessful investments be handled so losses are recognized rather than converted into permanent subsidies?

### What Are We Missing?

What risks, contradictions, unintended consequences, legal barriers, economic flaws, or better alternatives have we failed to consider?
