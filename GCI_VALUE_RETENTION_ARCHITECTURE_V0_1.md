# GCI Value Retention Architecture — V0.1

**Generational Capital Investment (GCI)**  
**Status:** Counterfactual institutional-design framework  
**Companion document:** `APOLLO_TAXPAYER_VALUE_LEAKAGE_FINDINGS_V0_1.md`

---

## Executive Summary

Apollo research has revealed a recurring institutional pattern:

> **Public capital can materially create, mature, validate, scale, or de-risk commercially valuable technology while the public financial interest disappears as that value moves into private commercialization.**

Private markets routinely protect economic interests through royalties, licenses, sublicensing provisions, equity, warrants, grant-back rights, patent sales, retained future participation, change-of-control clauses, and audit rights.

The central design question for Generational Capital Investment is therefore not whether government should own private companies or confiscate private innovation.

It is:

> **What contractual and institutional architecture would allow taxpayers to retain a small, finite, predictable, and declining economic interest in value their capital materially helps create, while leaving private firms strong incentives to commercialize, improve, compete, and profit?**

This document translates the leakage points identified through Apollo into a proposed **value-retention architecture** for future generational investments.

It is not proposed statutory language and is not legal advice. It is a research framework to be pressure-tested.

---

## 1. Design Objective

A GCI value-retention system should accomplish six things simultaneously:

1. **Recognize public contribution.**
2. **Preserve private ownership and commercial incentives.**
3. **Allow technologies to diffuse and markets to compete.**
4. **Prevent public participation from becoming permanent or unlimited.**
5. **Carry the public economic interest through foreseeable transfer events.**
6. **Return proceeds to a protected reinvestment fund rather than treating them as ordinary annual revenue.**

The architecture should be simple enough to price in advance.

A company receiving GCI-supported capital should know the participation economics before accepting the funding.

The public right should therefore behave more like a known **cost of capital or technology royalty** than an unpredictable future tax.

---

## 2. Core Principle

> **If public capital takes material economic risk, the public should retain an appropriate economic interest in the value that public capital helps create.**

That principle does not imply equal ownership.

Private companies may contribute pre-existing IP, additional R&D, manufacturing capital, commercialization expense, distribution, marketing, customer acquisition, regulatory work, later innovation, and decades of operating risk.

Those contributions justify substantial private returns.

GCI asks only whether the public contribution should automatically become financially worth **zero** once the technology becomes commercially successful.

---

## 3. The Value-Retention Stack

A modern GCI agreement should consider several different economic instruments rather than relying on one universal royalty.

### Instrument A — Revenue Participation / Royalty

Best suited for identifiable technology, commercial products with measurable sales, licensed IP, software, process technology, and product families with traceable lineage.

**Working research range:** **4%–7% initial participation on directly qualifying commercial value.**

This is a modeling range, not a universal proposed rate.

The actual rate should depend on the percentage of public versus private contribution, maturity at the time of funding, amount of public capital at risk, exclusivity, expected market size, private follow-on investment, and competitive conditions.

The defining feature is not the starting percentage. It is that the rate **declines and terminates**.

### Instrument B — Equity or Warrants

Best suited for company-level scale-up capital, manufacturing facilities, loan guarantees, rescue capital, and first-of-a-kind commercial plants where product-level royalty attribution would be impractical.

### Instrument C — Licensing Participation

Best suited for patents, software, know-how packages, proprietary data, and technology-transfer arrangements.

A GCI agreement can require that a defined percentage of licensing and sublicensing proceeds follow the originating public economic interest.

### Instrument D — IP Sale Participation

Best suited for patent portfolio sales, know-how sales, technology carve-outs, and asset transfers.

A defined percentage of proceeds from qualifying IP dispositions returns to the GCI fund.

### Instrument E — Infrastructure / Asset Income

Best suited for transmission, energy infrastructure, ports, communications infrastructure, and other productive public-private physical assets where a royalty is not the natural instrument.

---

## 4. Leakage Control 1 — Define Rights Before the Check Is Written

Apollo frequently requires later reconstruction of who owned what.

A modern GCI program should establish the public economic interest **at award execution**.

Every qualifying award should identify:

- pre-existing private IP;
- publicly funded foreground IP;
- jointly developed IP;
- government-furnished technology;
- public-funded know-how;
- project data;
- manufacturing processes;
- public test and validation contributions;
- private follow-on investment.

The agreement should answer in advance:

> **If this project becomes commercially successful, what economic participation survives for the public?**

The right should not depend on reconstructing intent fifty years later.

---

## 5. Leakage Control 2 — Sublicensing Pass-Through

### Apollo lesson

Fairchild could license technology and receive royalties from Philco.

A public participation right that attaches only to Fairchild's own product sales would miss that value.

### GCI control

A qualifying public participation right should follow direct licenses, cross-licenses, sublicenses, technology-access fees, know-how transfers, patent pools, paid covenants not to sue, and economically equivalent rights transactions.

The company keeps most licensing value. A defined public share follows the transaction.

### Anti-avoidance principle

Changing the legal form of commercialization should not erase the public economic interest.

---

## 6. Leakage Control 3 — Derivative Products Need a Declining Lineage Rule

### Apollo lesson

Tylan, Delco, Fairchild, and Black & Decker demonstrate that later products may contain meaningful public-program contributions while also containing substantial new private innovation.

A permanent full royalty would overreach.

An immediate zero would surrender value too early.

### GCI control

Use documented **derivative generations**:

- **Generation 0:** directly funded or matured capability
- **Generation 1:** immediate commercial derivative
- **Generation 2:** transferred or substantially modified derivative
- **Generation 3+:** increasingly remote descendants
- **Attribution stop:** public participation becomes zero

A separate economic factor reduces the applicable participation rate as derivative distance increases.

Illustratively, a 4% time-based rate might become 4% at Generation 0/1, 2% at Generation 2, 1% at Generation 3, 0.4% at Generation 4, and zero later.

The numbers are illustrative.

The principle is:

> **Technological distance should reduce the claim, not automatically erase it at the first derivative.**

---

## 7. Leakage Control 4 — Time Decay

A public participation right should not last forever.

A working GCI model currently tests schedules in which the full initial participation applies during early commercialization, declines in later decades, and reaches zero after a finite period.

The commercial clock should generally begin when **qualifying commercial revenue begins**, not when research funding is first awarded.

Otherwise a technology requiring 15 or 20 years to commercialize could lose most of its participation period before earning its first dollar.

Time decay recognizes that the original public contribution becomes less economically dominant, private capital accumulates, new inventions intervene, and old rights should eventually clear.

---

## 8. Leakage Control 5 — Publicly Financed Refinement

### Apollo lesson

Black & Decker had cordless technology before Apollo.

Apollo nevertheless financed motor optimization and engineering learning that NASA later traced into commercial cordless products.

A patent-ownership-only system would treat the public contribution as zero.

### GCI control

Create a separate category for **publicly financed refinement**.

Qualifying contributions might include major efficiency gains, reliability improvements, manufacturing-process improvements, cost reductions, miniaturization, durability, safety, validation, scale-up, and technical assistance that resolves commercialization barriers.

A refinement claim should generally be smaller than a claim on technology created substantially through public funding.

But it need not be zero.

Possible structures include a lower starting royalty, shorter duration, smaller derivative factor, or capped return.

---

## 9. Leakage Control 6 — Know-How, Data, and Manufacturing Capability

### Apollo lesson

The fuel-cell consent judgment's definition of “Apollo Technology” included more than patents. It encompassed technical information such as blueprints, specifications, laboratory results, testing information, operating data, manufacturing information, and cost information.

Semiconductor cases similarly show value residing in manufacturing learning and reliability expertise.

### GCI control

Every award should separately identify:

- patent rights;
- software rights;
- technical-data rights;
- manufacturing know-how;
- trade-secret / process rights;
- government-funded validation data;
- government-furnished technical assistance.

The objective is not to claim employee knowledge as government property.

It is to avoid pretending that publicly financed productive capability has no value merely because it is not patented.

---

## 10. Leakage Control 7 — IP Sale and Change of Control

### Apollo lesson

The UTC/Ballard transaction demonstrates sophisticated private value retention: upfront consideration, equity, grant-back rights, and continuing future participation.

### GCI control

A public participation agreement should survive patent sale, IP portfolio sale, asset sale, merger, acquisition, corporate restructuring, spinout, and assignment to an affiliate.

Possible structures include:

- participation right follows the asset;
- acquirer assumes the obligation;
- public fund receives a defined transaction participation;
- company may buy out the remaining public interest using a predefined formula.

A company should not be able to eliminate the public right simply by transferring the qualifying asset to another entity.

---

## 11. Leakage Control 8 — Bankruptcy and Distressed Transfers

The agreement should specify what happens if the recipient fails, assets are sold in bankruptcy, IP is purchased by another company, or the technology is abandoned and later revived.

The public claim should not automatically disappear merely because the original company fails.

At the same time, the structure should not prevent productive assets from being transferred out of failed companies.

A limited, transferable economic interest is preferable to operational control.

---

## 12. Publicly Supported Downstream Purchases Must Be Separated

The fuel-cell research revealed another accounting risk.

If government funds the original technology and later government agencies purchase or subsidize the commercial derivative, counting those downstream purchases as independent private-market return can make the program appear more commercially successful than it really was.

A GCI ledger should therefore separate:

### Private-market revenue
Default qualifying category.

### Rights / IP monetization
Separately qualifying where attribution is established.

### Publicly supported downstream revenue
Visible, but normally excluded from the core private-commercialization return metric.

### Provisional orders / market opportunity
Never treated as realized revenue.

---

## 13. Royalty Base Matters as Much as Royalty Rate

A 4% royalty can mean very different things depending on what counts as revenue.

The agreement must define gross or net sales, transfer pricing, discounts, returns, bundled products, related-party sales, service revenue, software subscriptions, licensing income, and geographic scope.

GCI should therefore follow this discipline:

> **Be conservative about what revenue qualifies. Do not artificially undervalue the participation rate after qualification has been established.**

---

## 14. Caps, Floors, and Buyouts

Not every public participation right should remain open-ended.

Possible safeguards include:

### Return cap
Participation ends after the public fund receives an agreed multiple of qualifying public capital.

### Time cap
Participation ends after a fixed maximum period.

### De minimis floor
Very small derivative streams are ignored to reduce administrative burden.

### Buyout option
The company can extinguish the remaining public participation by paying a transparent formula-based buyout.

### Public upside kicker
Particularly risky programs could have higher upside participation if commercial success is extraordinary.

These mechanisms make the public right predictable enough to price into private business decisions.

---

## 15. Competitive Neutrality

A poorly designed GCI system could distort markets.

Safeguards should include standardized participation rules, transparent award criteria, no political selection of individual corporate winners, competitive procurement where appropriate, professional investment governance, published conflict-of-interest rules, prohibition on hidden side agreements, and comparable treatment of similarly situated recipients.

The objective is to capture value from **public risk capital**, not to create favored firms.

---

## 16. Open Technology and Public Return Are Not Mutually Exclusive

The Pratt & Whitney fuel-cell case shows a legitimate public goal: broad dissemination of important technology.

A public return mechanism should not require technology lock-up.

Possible structures include:

- low royalty + broad licensing;
- royalty-free research / public-interest use;
- commercial participation only;
- declining open-license structures.

This could support both **diffusion** and **public return**.

---

## 17. The Public Return Should Be Reinvested, Not Spent Automatically

A durable GCI structure would direct proceeds to a protected investment fund.

Possible inflows include royalties, licensing income, warrant proceeds, dividends, equity sales, infrastructure income, IP-sale participation, and revenue shares.

The fund would reinvest those proceeds into future generational missions.

The objective is:

> **Do not require each generation of public investment to begin entirely from zero.**

This is not a promise to solve the national debt or Social Security.

It is a mechanism for preserving productive public capital across generations.

---

## 18. Proposed Transaction Logic

A future GCI-supported project could follow this sequence.

### Step 1 — Define the public contribution

Document dollars, guarantees, facilities, technical assistance, government IP, data, testing, scale-up support, and procurement commitments.

### Step 2 — Define pre-existing private contribution

Document background IP, prior R&D, manufacturing assets, proprietary processes, and private capital already invested.

### Step 3 — Choose the participation instrument

Use royalty, revenue share, equity, warrant, licensing participation, asset income, or a combination.

### Step 4 — Establish the lineage rules

Define direct products, derivatives, sublicenses, affiliates, IP sales, successor owners, and attribution-stop conditions.

### Step 5 — Establish decay and termination

Define commercial start date, rate schedule, derivative factors, return cap, maximum duration, and buyout formula.

### Step 6 — Audit commercial results

Require only the reporting necessary to verify the participation base. Avoid operational micromanagement.

### Step 7 — Return proceeds to GCI fund

Receipts remain public capital available for reinvestment.

---

## 19. Illustrative Contract Concept

This is not legal drafting, but the economic intent could be stated plainly:

> In consideration for material public capital or technical support supplied under this program, the recipient agrees that qualifying commercial exploitation of defined program-supported technology will carry a limited public economic participation right. The right applies only to qualifying commercial value, declines over time and technological distance, follows defined licenses and asset transfers, recognizes documented private contribution, and terminates under predetermined conditions. Proceeds are returned to the Generational Capital Investment Fund for reinvestment.

The critical attributes are:

**known in advance**  
**limited**  
**auditable**  
**declining**  
**transfer-aware**  
**competition-compatible**  
**eventually zero**

---

## 20. What GCI Should Not Do

A credible architecture should explicitly reject:

- permanent government royalties on distant technologies;
- claims on unrelated corporate revenue;
- government ownership merely because a company once received a public contract;
- retroactive claims against historical companies;
- political discretion over individual royalty enforcement;
- hidden or negotiable favoritism;
- double-counting public purchases as private return;
- claims based only on personnel genealogy;
- treating all indirect economic benefits as financial ROI;
- preventing private firms from earning large returns on successful commercialization.

The objective is **value retention**, not value confiscation.

## 21. Research Questions Before Policy Design

1. How do participation rights affect contractor bids?
2. Would firms demand higher upfront prices to offset future royalties?
3. Which instruments create the least distortion?
4. How should public and private contribution shares be measured?
5. How are mixed products and bundled technologies handled?
6. What audit burden is tolerable?
7. How should small businesses be treated?
8. What participation caps preserve innovation incentives?
9. How should international commercialization be handled?
10. How should jointly funded federal/state/private programs allocate rights?
11. What constitutional, procurement, patent, tax, and appropriations issues arise?
12. Can the fund be insulated from political spending while remaining democratically accountable?
13. How should losses and failed investments be recognized?
14. Should some technologies deliberately remain royalty-free because diffusion itself is the dominant public return?
15. How should national-security technology be treated differently?

## 22. Modern Application

The Apollo leakage framework can now be tested against modern public-private technology programs.

A particularly useful candidate is large-scale infrastructure and clean-energy investment involving batteries, hydrogen, grid technology, advanced transmission, carbon capture, advanced nuclear, industrial decarbonization, energy cybersecurity, first-of-a-kind demonstrations, and manufacturing scale-up.

Modern programs offer much better evidence than Apollo because award amounts, recipients, private cost share, project objectives, and commercialization pathways can often be tracked digitally from the beginning.

The key question is:

> **Before public capital de-risks the next commercially valuable technology, what value-retention mechanism should be written into the agreement so the taxpayer's economic interest does not disappear at the first successful private transaction?**

---

## 23. Working Architecture Principle

The Apollo research suggests the following working principle:

> **Private markets should be free to commercialize, improve, sublicense, sell, and profit from publicly supported technology. But when public capital materially contributes to the commercial asset, a small and predictable public economic interest should travel with the qualifying value for a finite period, decline as private contribution and technological distance grow, and ultimately expire.**

The public right should be something a private firm can understand, price, plan around, account for, and buy out where appropriate.

That makes it part of the cost of capital rather than an unpredictable claim on future success.

---

## 24. Relationship to the Apollo Finding

The Apollo findings identify the leaks.

This architecture proposes the valves.

| Apollo leakage | GCI retention mechanism |
|---|---|
| IP/control transfer | Defined foreground-IP participation |
| Private sublicensing | Sublicense pass-through |
| Derivative commercialization | Derivative-distance participation |
| Know-how migration | Data/know-how classification and limited participation where appropriate |
| Later IP monetization | IP-sale and licensing participation |
| Publicly financed refinement | Reduced refinement-based participation |
| Corporate acquisition | Successor/change-of-control continuation |
| Technology diffusion | Broad licensing with modest commercial participation |
| Long time horizon | Time decay and termination |
| Lost public proceeds | Protected reinvestment fund |

The objective is not to stop technology transfer.

It is to stop **financial value from disappearing merely because technology transfer succeeded.**

---

## 25. Status

This document is a research architecture, not a policy recommendation.

It should be challenged on economics, law, incentives, administration, competitive effects, innovation effects, international implications, governance, and political capture risk.

The next phase should test whether the proposed value-retention mechanisms would have been workable in the historical Apollo cases and how they would operate against modern public technology investments.
